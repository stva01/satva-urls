1 · What is going on

Normal embeddings are unreadable

A normal (“dense”) embedding turns text into hundreds of numbers like 0.13, −0.87, 0.42… They work well for search, but no human can look at them and say what the text is about, or why two texts matched.

QA embeddings are a questionnaire

Here, each position in the vector is a question. Position 36 is always “Does the contract cap liability?” — 1 = yes, 0 = no. Read the vector left to right and you are reading a completed checklist about that contract.

Why contracts? CUAD already did the hard part

CUAD is 510 real commercial contracts filed with the US SEC. Lawyers and trained law students read every one and marked 41 types of clauses — liability caps, termination rights, non-competes, governing law, licences, and so on — over 13,000 annotations in total. Those 41 clause types become our 41 questions, and the lawyers’ marks become the ground truth we score against. We did not invent the questions; lawyers did.

What the pilot actually did

  1. Take 10 contracts. The first 10 in CUAD — from a 1,081-character joint filing agreement to a 175,580-character pharma promotion agreement.
  2. Ask all 41 questions about each one. 10 × 41 = 410 answers. For every “yes”, the model also had to quote the exact clause as evidence — no evidence, no credit for a confident guess.
  3. That list of answers is the embedding. A 41-bit vector per contract, e.g. [1, 1, 1, 1, 1, 1, 0, 1, …].
  4. Score it against the lawyers. Correct, false alarm (said yes, lawyers said no), or missed (said no, lawyers said yes).
  5. Try searching with it. A lawyer’s request gets its own, complementary set of questions — see the worked example below.
Why “asymmetric”? A contract states things; a search query asks for things. Asking both sides the identical questions fails — a query saying “find contracts we can walk away from” does not itself contain a termination clause. So the query side uses paired questions in the asker’s shape (“Am I asking about ending the contract early?”) lined up position-by-position with the contract side (“Can a party terminate without cause?”). Same idea, different shape — then the two vectors can be compared.

How close are they? Three ways to measure, one that matters most here

  • Coverage — did the contract satisfy everything the query asked for? Query asked for 2 things. Contract #4 has both → coverage 1.0. Contract #1 has only one of the two in the model’s answers (it predicted a termination-for-convenience clause, but no liability cap) → coverage 0.5. For a lawyer, this is the headline number: “show me only contracts that tick every box I asked for.”
  • Cosine similarity — the standard angle between two vectors. Contract #4: 0.447. Contract #1: 0.154.
  • Jaccard — shared yeses ÷ all yeses on either side. Contract #4: 0.200. Contract #1: 0.045. It looks harsh because it also penalises a contract for having extra clauses nobody asked about.

All three are ordinary, off-the-shelf measures — no new search algorithm is needed. Binary vectors like these are exactly what tools such as FAISS already index at scale. The research work is in the questions and their pairing, not in the distance maths.

The retrieval test — the six query vectors

Not run yet. The retrieval test itself has not been run. The six vectors below are the inputs the test will use — written out in full here so every bit can be checked and corrected before anything runs. No retrieval scores are claimed on this page yet.

The pilot so far scored the answering step: whether each bit of each contract’s embedding matches the lawyers’ labels. That is not the same as testing whether those embeddings actually retrieve the right contracts. This test is designed to answer that second question: take six lawyer-style requests, turn each one into a query vector, rank the 10 contracts against it, and check the ranking against the lawyers’ ground truth.

The rule for building a query vector

A bit is 1 only if the query explicitly asks for that clause. Silence is 0. If the words of the query do not ask for a clause, that position stays 0 — no guessing, no “probably relevant” bits.

Only the 32 yes/no dimensions are used. The other 9 of the 41 categories — the value and metadata ones (document name, parties, agreement date, effective date, expiration date, renewal term, notice period, governing law, warranty duration) — are dropped for this test, because they appear in nearly every contract and would swamp the similarity: almost every contract would look alike on those bits, drowning out the clauses the query is actually about.

Why these vectors are hand-built, in one visible step

Each query vector below was written in a single, visible step: read the query, decide which of the 32 dimensions it explicitly asks for, write those bits down with the reason next to each one. The alternative — sending the query through 32 separate question-answering calls — would hide every decision inside its own black box, where a bit could flip for a reason nobody can see.

Hand-built does not remove judgement; it makes the judgement auditable. Every 1 below carries its reason, so a wrong bit can be spotted and corrected. It also keeps the test clean: with the query side fixed and inspectable, any retrieval miss can only come from the contract embeddings — the passage side — which is exactly what this test is trying to measure, instead of mixing in query-side answering errors as well.

Two judgment calls, stated plainly.

Q3 — License Grant is a 1. A licence has to be granted before it can be irrevocable. Q3 asks for “a licence that can’t be taken away,” so both the grant (dim 17) and the irrevocable/perpetual quality (dim 22) are part of what is being asked for.

Q1 — “maximum payout is limited” is Cap On Liability, not Liquidated Damages. A cap limits how much can be owed; liquidated damages are a pre-agreed amount payable on breach. Those are different clauses, so dim 27 (Cap On Liability) is 1 and dim 28 (Liquidated Damages) stays 0.

If any bit above looks wrong, it can be changed before the test runs — that is the point of writing the vectors out here first.

5 · Results & what’s next

Headline: accuracy 0.917 vs 0.673 for always answering “no” · precision 0.866 · recall 0.879 · F1 0.872 · macro-F1 0.788 · 116 true positives, 18 false alarms, 16 missed, 260 true negatives · evidence quotes verbatim in the contract text 128/134 (95.5%).

What went wrong — and it is specific, which is the point

  • Post-Termination Services over-fired (6 of the 18 false alarms). The model counted almost any duty surviving termination; CUAD’s annotators used a stricter definition. Fix: tighten that one question’s wording.
  • Expiration Date was missed (6 of the 16 misses). Expiry is often written as a term length — “ten (10) years from…” — not as a calendar date, so the model did not recognise it. Fix: say so explicitly in the question.
  • The longest contracts were weakest. Contracts #9 and #10 (175,580 and 145,168 chars) scored 0.854 accuracy, the joint-lowest. Fix at scale: split long contracts into chunks, answer per chunk, and mark a clause present if any chunk says yes.

The honest caveat

This pilot measures clause presence — is the clause there, yes or no? CUAD’s original benchmark task is harder: find the exact words of the clause. So do not compare this 0.872 F1 directly with the original paper’s DeBERTa result (44% precision at 80% recall on exact-span extraction). Different task, different difficulty. The fair comparison for the full run is other systems on this same presence task.

Next step

Scale the same method to all 510 contracts, with the two question fixes above and chunking for long contracts. Generation is the cheap part; the value is the per-category accuracy table and the retrieval test — a readable embedding that can show a lawyer which questions fired, and the exact clause behind each one.

6 · Every metric, explained

Both projects use the same trick — turn text into answers to plain yes/no questions, and use those answers as the embedding — but they were scored in completely different ways. This section puts every metric from both side by side. Click or tap any metric to expand what it actually means.

Read this first — why you can’t rank these numbers against each other.

These numbers measure different things. Ours ask: did we say “yes” and “no” in the same places the lawyers did, and do the vectors rank contracts sensibly? The fMRI paper mostly asks: how well do the answers predict real brain activity? — plus one document-ranking test. Predicting brain activity, saying whether a clause is present, and ranking documents are three different jobs with three different levels of difficulty. A bigger number in one paper does not mean a better method — 0.917 and 0.116 are not two scores in the same race.

Our pilot — CUAD, 10 contracts, 410 pairs

Scored by comparing each of the 410 answers (10 contracts × 41 questions) with the lawyers’ labels in CUAD, plus the similarity measures from the worked example in section 2.

Accuracy — tap to explain0.917

What it measures: the share of all answers that were right — both the “yes” answers that should have been yes and the “no” answers that should have been no.

What 0.917 means: out of every 100 questions, about 92 answers were right. It is the simplest headline score, but on its own it can flatter a system when one answer (“no”) is much more common than the other — which is why the baseline below matters.

Precision — tap to explain0.866

What it measures: when the model speaks up and says “yes, this clause is here”, how often is it telling the truth? It punishes false alarms.

What 0.866 means: of the times the model said “yes”, the lawyers agreed 86.6% of the time. The rest were false alarms — clauses the model claimed to find that the lawyers said are not there.

Recall — tap to explain0.879

What it measures: of everything that is really there to be found, how much did the model actually find? It punishes misses — the mirror image of precision.

What 0.879 means: of all the clauses the lawyers actually found, the model caught 87.9%. The rest slipped past it and were wrongly answered “no”.

F1 — tap to explain0.872

What it measures: one number balancing precision and recall, so a system cannot score well by only being careful (high precision, misses a lot) or only being eager (high recall, cries wolf a lot). It is the standard single score for this kind of yes/no labelling task.

What 0.872 means: precision and recall are both fairly high and close together, so their balance lands at 0.872 — a good, even performance rather than one propping up the other.

Macro-F1 — tap to explain0.788

What it measures: F1 worked out separately for each of the 41 clause categories, then averaged — so rare categories count as much as common ones. The headline F1 above, by contrast, pools all 410 answers together, which lets frequent, easy categories dominate.

What 0.788 means: it is lower than the pooled F1 (0.872), which tells you the model does worse on some rarer or harder categories than the headline suggests. Macro-F1 is the stricter, fairer-to-the-tail score.

All-“no” baseline accuracy — tap to explain0.673

What it measures: the accuracy of the laziest possible system — one that answers “no” to every single question without reading anything. Because most clauses are absent from most contracts, doing nothing already looks deceptively decent.

What 0.673 means: this is the floor our numbers must beat. Our accuracy of 0.917 is only meaningful because it sits well above this 0.673 — the gap between them is the real signal the reading adds.

Evidence verbatim — tap to explain95.5%

What it measures: trustworthiness of the receipts. For every “yes”, the model had to quote the exact clause as evidence; this checks how often that quote was copied word-for-word from the contract, rather than paraphrased or invented.

What 95.5% means: the quoted clause was verbatim from the contract in 128 of 134 cases. A readable embedding is only useful if its explanations can be trusted, and this is the check on that.

Coverage — tap to explain1.0 = all · 0.5 = half

What it measures: a search score, not a labelling score. For a given query, did this contract satisfy everything the query asked for? It only looks at the positions the query cares about and ignores the rest of the vector.

What the numbers mean: 1.0 means the contract ticks every box the query asked for, 0.5 means it ticks half of them, and 0 means it ticks none. For a lawyer this is often the headline: “show me only contracts that satisfy everything I asked for.” See the ranking table in section 2 for it in action.

Cosine similarity — tap to explain1 = same direction

What it measures: how alike two embedding vectors are in direction — the standard way of comparing embeddings. It cares about the pattern of answers, not how long either vector’s list of “yes” answers is.

What the numbers mean: 1 means the two vectors are pointing the same way — the same pattern of yeses. Nearer to 0 means the patterns have little in common. Higher is more similar; it is a ranking score for search, not a right/wrong score.

Jaccard — tap to explainoverlap ÷ union

What it measures: the overlap of the two sets of “yes” answers divided by their union — how many yeses they share, out of every position where either of them said yes.

What the numbers mean: 1 would mean identical sets of yeses; 0 means no shared yes at all. Jaccard tends to look harsh next to cosine and coverage, because it also penalises a contract for having extra clauses nobody asked about.

Embedding bit agreement — tap to explain91.7%

What it measures: the fraction of the 41 bits in each embedding that are correct, compared bit-by-bit with the embedding you would build from the lawyers’ labels.

What 91.7% means: it is the same number as accuracy (0.917), framed differently — as “how much of the vector is right”. Accuracy talks about answers; bit agreement talks about the embedding itself. If roughly 92% of the bits are right, the vector as a whole is a faithful, readable summary of the contract.

Retrieval check — tap to explain9 / 10

What it measures: the first — and so far only — direct test of whether the embeddings work for search. Take the one example query from section 2, rank all 10 contracts using our predicted embeddings, then rank them again using embeddings built from the lawyers’ labels, and count how often the two rankings agree on a contract’s position.

What 9/10 means: the ranking from our embeddings matched the ranking from the lawyers’ labels on 9 of 10 contracts. That is a promising hint, not proof — it is one query and ten documents. A real retrieval test needs several lawyer-style queries with known relevant contracts, scored with measures like the MRR below.

The fMRI paper — Benara et al., NeurIPS 2024

Benara et al., “Crafting Interpretable Embeddings for Language Neuroscience by Asking LLMs Questions”, NeurIPS 2024 — the paper this pilot borrows its idea from. Instead of contracts, they embedded language: GPT-4 generated ~674 yes/no questions, an LLM answered them about podcast stories people listened to in a scanner, and those answers were used to predict brain activity. Setup: 3 subjects, 20+ hours of podcast stories, 79 of 82 stories for training and 2 held out for testing, and ~100k voxels (tiny brain-volume units) reduced with PCA to 100.

Average test correlation — tap to explain0.116

What it measures: how well the embedding predicted real brain activity on the held-out stories the model had never seen — do the predicted ups and downs of brain activity track the measured ones?

What 0.116 means: 1.0 would be perfect prediction and 0 would be nothing at all, so 0.116 looks tiny — but brain signals are noisy, so even the best models land low. In this field, small correlations that reliably beat strong baselines are the result; the number only makes sense next to the comparisons below.

vs Eng1000 (hand-built features) — tap to explain+26%

What it measures: a head-to-head on the same brain-prediction task against Eng1000, the best hand-built interpretable feature set — 985 features written by hand by researchers.

What +26% means: the question-based embedding beat those 985 hand-written features by 26% in relative terms. Questions an LLM thought up outperformed features experts spent years crafting — a core claim of the paper.

vs BERT — tap to explain+5%

What it measures: the same brain-prediction comparison, this time against embeddings from BERT, a standard (unreadable) language model.

What +5% means: the readable, question-based embedding predicted brain activity 5% better than BERT’s mystery numbers — evidence that interpretability did not have to cost performance here.

vs best LLaMA — tap to explain−7%

What it measures: the same comparison against the best-performing LLaMA embeddings — the strongest unreadable baseline in the paper.

What −7% means: the question-based embedding came in 7% below the best LLaMA embeddings. That gap is the price of being readable in this paper: you give up a little predictive power, and in return every dimension means something a human can read.

Elastic Net, just 29 questions — tap to explain0.122 vs 0.118

What it measures: how few questions you can get away with. An Elastic Net model picked out just 29 of the ~674 questions, and brain activity was predicted using only those.

What 0.122 vs 0.118 means: with just 29 questions, the correlation was 0.122 — slightly beating all 985 hand-built Eng1000 features at 0.118. Most of the signal lives in a small, nameable subset of questions, which is exactly what makes the embedding interpretable in practice, not just in principle.

Distilled RoBERTa agreement — tap to explain88.5%

What it measures: distillation — training a small, cheap model (RoBERTa) to copy the big model’s yes/no answers, then checking how often the small model gives the same answer as the big one.

What 88.5% means: the small model agreed with the big model 88.5% of the time. Answering ~674 questions with a large model for every piece of text is expensive; a small copier that is right nearly nine times in ten makes the approach practical.

Distilled correlations — tap to explain0.111 / 0.113 vs 0.114

What it measures: whether the cheap distilled model keeps the expensive model’s brain-prediction power. Two flavours were tried: binary answers (plain yes/no) and probabilistic answers (how confident the small model is).

What the numbers mean: the distilled models scored 0.111 (binary) and 0.113 (probabilistic) against 0.114 for the original — the small distilled model kept almost all of the brain-prediction power, losing only a sliver in exchange for being far cheaper to run.

Retrieval MRR — QA-Emb vs BM-25 — tap to explain0.45 vs 0.77 · combined 0.80

What it measures: document ranking, scored with MRR (mean reciprocal rank) — how high the right document lands in a ranked list. 1.0 means the right document is always first; the lower it lands, the lower the score. This is the fMRI paper’s one test that is the same kind of job as our contract search.

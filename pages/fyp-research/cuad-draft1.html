Can you read an embedding?
10 contracts, 41 yes/no questions.

Instead of turning a contract into 768 mystery numbers, we asked an AI 41 plain questions about it — “Does it cap liability? Can it be ended early?” — and used the yes/no answers as the embedding. Every number can be read, checked, and argued with. This page shows the whole pilot: the contracts, the questions, the embeddings, a search query and its embedding, and the score against lawyers’ labels.

What changed in v2

The first run (v1, 16 Nov 2026) exposed three fixable problems. The same 10 contracts were then re-answered (v2, 25 Nov 2026) with four changes — and nothing else moved: same contracts, same lawyers’ labels, same scoring.

  • Six question definitions were rewritten. Post-Termination Services now requires services or support that continue after the contract ends — just owing money, repaying money, or returning or destroying materials no longer counts. Expiration Date now counts a term length (“ten years from the Effective Date”) as an expiry, not only a calendar date. Non-Transferable License and Anti-Assignment were told apart: one is about transferring the licence itself, the other about handing over the whole contract. And the two affiliate-licence questions now state the direction plainly — a licence coming from the licensor’s affiliates is a different answer from one going to the licensee’s affiliates.
  • Every “yes” now needs a word-for-word quote. If the exact words cannot be found in the contract, the answer is “no”.
  • Long contracts are split into chunks of about 20,000 characters. Each chunk is answered separately and the answers are combined — a clause counts as present if any chunk finds it.
  • Ranking is now coverage-first. Contracts that match everything the query asked for come first; cosine similarity only orders contracts within the same coverage.

Every number on this page is v2 unless it is labelled v1.

1 · What is going on

Normal embeddings are unreadable

A normal (“dense”) embedding turns text into hundreds of numbers like 0.13, −0.87, 0.42… They work well for search, but no human can look at them and say what the text is about, or why two texts matched.

QA embeddings are a questionnaire

Here, each position in the vector is a question. Position 36 is always “Does the contract cap liability?” — 1 = yes, 0 = no. Read the vector left to right and you are reading a completed checklist about that contract.

Why contracts? CUAD already did the hard part

CUAD is 510 real commercial contracts filed with the US SEC. Lawyers and trained law students read every one and marked 41 types of clauses — liability caps, termination rights, non-competes, governing law, licences, and so on — over 13,000 annotations in total. Those 41 clause types become our 41 questions, and the lawyers’ marks become the ground truth we score against. We did not invent the questions; lawyers did.

What the pilot actually did

  1. Take 10 contracts. The first 10 in CUAD — from a 1,081-character joint filing agreement to a 175,580-character pharma promotion agreement.
  2. Ask all 41 questions about each one. 10 × 41 = 410 answers. For every “yes”, the model also had to quote the exact clause as evidence — no evidence, no credit for a confident guess.
  3. That list of answers is the embedding. A 41-bit vector per contract, e.g. [1, 1, 1, 1, 1, 1, 0, 1, …].
  4. Score it against the lawyers. Correct, false alarm (said yes, lawyers said no), or missed (said no, lawyers said yes).
  5. Try searching with it. A lawyer’s request gets its own, complementary set of questions — see the worked example below.
Why “asymmetric”? A contract states things; a search query asks for things. Asking both sides the identical questions fails — a query saying “find contracts we can walk away from” does not itself contain a termination clause. So the query side uses paired questions in the asker’s shape (“Am I asking about ending the contract early?”) lined up position-by-position with the contract side (“Can a party terminate without cause?”). Same idea, different shape — then the two vectors can be compared.

How close are they? Three ways to measure, one that matters most here

  • Coverage — did the contract satisfy everything the query asked for? Query asked for 2 things. Contract #4 has both → coverage 1.0. Contract #1 has neither in the v2 answers → coverage 0.0. In v1 it scored 0.5, because the model wrongly claimed a termination-for-convenience clause; the fixed question removed that false alarm, and there is no liability cap either. For a lawyer, this is the headline number: “show me only contracts that tick every box I asked for.”
  • Cosine similarity — the standard angle between two vectors, measured here on the 32 yes/no dimensions (the same basis as the retrieval test in the next section). Contract #4: 1.000 — on those dimensions it says yes to exactly the two things asked for, and nothing else. Contract #1: 0.000.
  • Jaccard — shared yeses ÷ all yeses on either side, also on the 32 yes/no dimensions. Contract #4: 1.000. Contract #1: 0.000. It usually looks harsh because it also penalises a contract for having extra clauses nobody asked about.

All three are ordinary, off-the-shelf measures — no new search algorithm is needed. Binary vectors like these are exactly what tools such as FAISS already index at scale. The research work is in the questions and their pairing, not in the distance maths.

The retrieval test — the six query vectors

Re-run — 25 Nov 2026 (v2). Same six hand-built query vectors (below) and the same strict relevance — a contract must have both categories the query asks for, in the lawyers’ ground truth. Two things changed: the contract embeddings are the v2 answers, and ranking is now coverage-first (coverage on the 32 yes/no dimensions, then cosine, ties broken by contract index). Headline result: ours v2 — Precision@3 0.834, MRR 1.000 — exactly equal to the oracle ceiling (lawyers’ own labels, same ranking rule). Ours v1 (cosine only) was P@3 0.78, MRR 0.89. Random — P@3 0.75. Full per-query results are in the table at the end of this section. The pilot is end to end: answering and retrieval have both been measured, twice.

The pilot first scored the answering step: whether each bit of each contract’s embedding matches the lawyers’ labels. That is not the same as testing whether those embeddings actually retrieve the right contracts. This test answers that second question: take six lawyer-style requests, turn each one into a query vector, rank the 10 contracts against it, and check the ranking against the lawyers’ ground truth.

The rule for building a query vector

A bit is 1 only if the query explicitly asks for that clause. Silence is 0. If the words of the query do not ask for a clause, that position stays 0 — no guessing, no “probably relevant” bits.

Only the 32 yes/no dimensions are used. The other 9 of the 41 categories — the value and metadata ones (document name, parties, agreement date, effective date, expiration date, renewal term, notice period, governing law, warranty duration) — are dropped for this test, because they appear in nearly every contract and would swamp the similarity: almost every contract would look alike on those bits, drowning out the clauses the query is actually about.

Why these vectors are hand-built, in one visible step

Each query vector below was written in a single, visible step: read the query, decide which of the 32 dimensions it explicitly asks for, write those bits down with the reason next to each one. The alternative — sending the query through 32 separate question-answering calls — would hide every decision inside its own black box, where a bit could flip for a reason nobody can see.

Hand-built does not remove judgement; it makes the judgement auditable. Every 1 below carries its reason, so a wrong bit can be spotted and corrected. It also keeps the test clean: with the query side fixed and inspectable, any retrieval miss can only come from the contract embeddings — the passage side — which is exactly what this test is trying to measure, instead of mixing in query-side answering errors as well.

What the results actually say — plain words

  • MRR is the real signal — and v2 maxes it. Ours is 1.000, against about 0.45 for random. The first right answer is at rank 1 on all 6 queries, exactly matching the oracle. V1 was 0.89, rank 1 on 5 of 6. The embeddings now put a correct contract first every time — on this test.
  • P@3 is at its ceiling — which still proves less than it looks. Ours is 0.834, and 0.834 is the highest mean P@3 any ranking can reach here: Q3, Q5 and Q6 have only 2 strictly relevant contracts each, so even perfect answers average 0.834. Random is 0.75. Matching the ceiling is the best possible outcome on this test, but with only 10 contracts P@3 still cannot tell a good system from luck the way it could at 510. Do not quote 0.834 vs 0.75 as a win either.
  • Q5 is fixed. P@3 0.67, MRR 1.00 — in v1 it was 0.33 / 0.33, the worst row in the table. Q5 was built to stress-test the Post-Termination Services over-firing. The rewritten question stopped the false alarms: contract 7, which has neither category in the ground truth and ranked 2nd in v1 on a single false-alarm bit, is out of the top 3, and a contract that genuinely has both clauses ranks 1st.
  • Cosine alone got worse in v2 — coverage-first is what makes retrieval work. Rank the v2 embeddings by cosine alone and Q1 drops to P@3 0.667: contract 2, which matches only one of the two asked-for clauses, pushes into the top 3. Coverage-first restores Q1 to 1.00. Cosine on these sparse vectors is fragile — v2 makes that clearer, not weaker — and requiring every asked-for bit first is the fix, not a nicety.
  • Q4 looks perfect, but read it carefully. Q4 still scores 1.00 / 1.00 despite the still-missed Change Of Control clause in contract 8. Contract 8 still ranks 3rd on its Audit Rights bit alone. So a perfect score here does not mean nothing was missed — it means the miss did not cost a top-3 place this time. The same pattern helps Q6.
  • Our embeddings now equal the ceiling. Ours v2 0.834 / 1.000 vs oracle 0.834 / 1.000 — identical scores, and the same top-3 sets. In v1 there was a gap (0.78 / 0.89 vs 0.83 / 0.92 with cosine). Closing it came from both halves of the fix: better answers and a better ranking rule.

Bottom line: with coverage-first ranking, the v2 embeddings retrieve exactly as well as embeddings built from the lawyers’ own labels — on all six queries. That is a pilot-scale result on 10 contracts, not a proof: P@3 sits at its ceiling here and cannot discriminate further, and there is still no keyword or dense-retrieval baseline for these embeddings to beat. The weaknesses are named in section 7.

  • The longest contracts were weakest — half fixed. Contracts #9 and #10 (175,580 and 145,168 chars) both scored 0.854 accuracy in v1, the joint-lowest. With chunking, #10 improved to 0.927 — but #9 is unchanged at 0.854, still the weakest contract on the page. Chunking did not help it.

The honest caveat

This pilot measures clause presence — is the clause there, yes or no? CUAD’s original benchmark task is harder: find the exact words of the clause. So do not compare this 0.906 F1 directly with the original paper’s DeBERTa result (44% precision at 80% recall on exact-span extraction). Different task, different difficulty. The fair comparison for the full run is other systems on this same presence task.

Next step

The fixes are done and measured, so the full run is now unblocked: scale the same method — v2 questions, chunking, coverage-first ranking — to all 510 contracts. It has not been started; it is waiting on a go-ahead. Generation is the cheap part; the value is the per-category accuracy table and the retrieval test — a readable embedding that can show a lawyer which questions fired, and the exact clause behind each one.

6 · Every metric, explained

Both projects use the same trick — turn text into answers to plain yes/no questions, and use those answers as the embedding — but they were scored in completely different ways. This section puts every metric from both side by side. Click or tap any metric to expand what it actually means.

Read this first — why you can’t rank these numbers against each other.

These numbers measure different things. Ours ask: did we say “yes” and “no” in the same places the lawyers did, and do the vectors rank contracts sensibly? The fMRI paper mostly asks: how well do the answers predict real brain activity? — plus one document-ranking test. Predicting brain activity, saying whether a clause is present, and ranking documents are three different jobs with three different levels of difficulty. A bigger number in one paper does not mean a better method — 0.939 and 0.116 are not two scores in the same race.

Our pilot — CUAD, 10 contracts, 410 pairs

Scored by comparing each of the 410 answers (10 contracts × 41 questions) with the lawyers’ labels in CUAD, plus the similarity measures from the worked example in section 2.

Accuracy — tap to explain0.939 · v1 0.917

What it measures: the share of all answers that were right — both the “yes” answers that should have been yes and the “no” answers that should have been no.

What 0.939 means: out of every 100 questions, about 94 answers were right (v1: about 92). It is the simplest headline score, but on its own it can flatter a system when one answer (“no”) is much more common than the other — which is why the baseline below matters.

Precision — tap to explain0.902 · v1 0.866

What it measures: when the model speaks up and says “yes, this clause is here”, how often is it telling the truth? It punishes false alarms.

What 0.902 means: of the times the model said “yes”, the lawyers agreed 90.2% of the time (v1: 86.6%). The rest were false alarms — clauses the model claimed to find that the lawyers said are not there.

Recall — tap to explain0.909 · v1 0.879

What it measures: of everything that is really there to be found, how much did the model actually find? It punishes misses — the mirror image of precision.

What 0.909 means: of all the clauses the lawyers actually found, the model caught 90.9% (v1: 87.9%). The rest slipped past it and were wrongly answered “no”.

F1 — tap to explain0.906 · v1 0.872

What it measures: one number balancing precision and recall, so a system cannot score well by only being careful (high precision, misses a lot) or only being eager (high recall, cries wolf a lot). It is the standard single score for this kind of yes/no labelling task.

What 0.906 means: precision and recall are both fairly high and close together, so their balance lands at 0.906 (v1: 0.872) — a good, even performance rather than one propping up the other.

Macro-F1 — tap to explain0.807 · v1 0.788

What it measures: F1 worked out separately for each of the 41 clause categories, then averaged — so rare categories count as much as common ones. The headline F1 above, by contrast, pools all 410 answers together, which lets frequent, easy categories dominate.

What 0.807 means: it is lower than the pooled F1 (0.906), which tells you the model does worse on some rarer or harder categories than the headline suggests. Macro-F1 is the stricter, fairer-to-the-tail score — and it rose from 0.788 in v1, so the v2 improvement was not only in the common categories.

All-“no” baseline accuracy — tap to explain0.673

What it measures: the accuracy of the laziest possible system — one that answers “no” to every single question without reading anything. Because most clauses are absent from most contracts, doing nothing already looks deceptively decent.

What 0.673 means: this is the floor our numbers must beat. Our accuracy of 0.939 is only meaningful because it sits well above this 0.673 — the gap between them is the real signal the reading adds.

Evidence verbatim — tap to explain96.2% · v1 95.5%

What it measures: trustworthiness of the receipts. For every “yes”, the model had to quote the exact clause as evidence; this checks how often that quote was copied word-for-word from the contract, rather than paraphrased or invented.

What 96.2% means: the quoted clause was verbatim from the contract in 128 of 133 cases (v1: 128 of 134, 95.5%). A readable embedding is only useful if its explanations can be trusted, and this is the check on that.

Coverage — tap to explain1.0 = all · 0.5 = half

What it measures: a search score, not a labelling score. For a given query, did this contract satisfy everything the query asked for? It only looks at the positions the query cares about and ignores the rest of the vector.

What the numbers mean: 1.0 means the contract ticks every box the query asked for, 0.5 means it ticks half of them, and 0 means it ticks none. For a lawyer this is often the headline: “show me only contracts that satisfy everything I asked for.” See the ranking table in section 2 for it in action.

Cosine similarity — tap to explain1 = same direction

What it measures: how alike two embedding vectors are in direction — the standard way of comparing embeddings. It cares about the pattern of answers, not how long either vector’s list of “yes” answers is.

What the numbers mean: 1 means the two vectors are pointing the same way — the same pattern of yeses. Nearer to 0 means the patterns have little in common. Higher is more similar; it is a ranking score for search, not a right/wrong score.

Jaccard — tap to explainoverlap ÷ union

What it measures: the overlap of the two sets of “yes” answers divided by their union — how many yeses they share, out of every position where either of them said yes.

What the numbers mean: 1 would mean identical sets of yeses; 0 means no shared yes at all. Jaccard tends to look harsh next to cosine and coverage, because it also penalises a contract for having extra clauses nobody asked about.

Embedding bit agreement — tap to explain93.9% · v1 91.7%

What it measures: the fraction of the 41 bits in each embedding that are correct, compared bit-by-bit with the embedding you would build from the lawyers’ labels.

What 93.9% means: it is the same number as accuracy (0.939), framed differently — as “how much of the vector is right”. Accuracy talks about answers; bit agreement talks about the embedding itself. If roughly 94% of the bits are right, the vector as a whole is a faithful, readable summary of the contract.

Retrieval — P@3 and MRR — tap to explain0.834 · 1.000

What they measure: whether the embeddings actually work for search. Six lawyer-style queries (the retrieval section above) each rank all 10 contracts. P@3 = how many of the top 3 are strictly relevant; MRR = how high the first strictly relevant contract lands — 1.000 means it is at rank 1 on every query.

What the v2 numbers mean: P@3 0.834 and MRR 1.000 — exactly equal to the oracle, the scores from embeddings built out of the lawyers’ own labels. V1, ranked by cosine alone, scored P@3 0.78 and MRR 0.89. Two cautions: 0.834 is the highest P@3 this test allows (some queries have only 2 strictly relevant contracts), and 10 contracts is too few for P@3 to separate skill from luck — random already scores 0.75.

The fMRI paper — Benara et al., NeurIPS 2024

Benara et al., “Crafting Interpretable Embeddings for Language Neuroscience by Asking LLMs Questions”, NeurIPS 2024 — the paper this pilot borrows its idea from. Instead of contracts, they embedded language: GPT-4 generated ~674 yes/no questions, an LLM answered them about podcast stories people listened to in a scanner, and those answers were used to predict brain activity. Setup: 3 subjects, 20+ hours of podcast stories, 79 of 82 stories for training and 2 held out for testing, and ~100k voxels (tiny brain-volume units) reduced with PCA to 100.

Average test correlation — tap to explain0.116

What it measures: how well the embedding predicted real brain activity on the held-out stories the model had never seen — do the predicted ups and downs of brain activity track the measured ones?

What 0.116 means: 1.0 would be perfect prediction and 0 would be nothing at all, so 0.116 looks tiny — but brain signals are noisy, so even the best models land low. In this field, small correlations that reliably beat strong baselines are the result; the number only makes sense next to the comparisons below.

vs Eng1000 (hand-built features) — tap to explain+26%

What it measures: a head-to-head on the same brain-prediction task against Eng1000, the best hand-built interpretable feature set — 985 features written by hand by researchers.

What +26% means: the question-based embedding beat those 985 hand-written features by 26% in relative terms. Questions an LLM thought up outperformed features experts spent years crafting — a core claim of the paper.

vs BERT — tap to explain+5%

What it measures: the same brain-prediction comparison, this time against embeddings from BERT, a standard (unreadable) language model.

What +5% means: the readable, question-based embedding predicted brain activity 5% better than BERT’s mystery numbers — evidence that interpretability did not have to cost performance here.

vs best LLaMA — tap to explain−7%

What it measures: the same comparison against the best-performing LLaMA embeddings — the strongest unreadable baseline in the paper.

What −7% means: the question-based embedding came in 7% below the best LLaMA embeddings. That gap is the price of being readable in this paper: you give up a little predictive power, and in return every dimension means something a human can read.

Elastic Net, just 29 questions — tap to explain0.122 vs 0.118

What it measures: how few questions you can get away with. An Elastic Net model picked out just 29 of the ~674 questions, and brain activity was predicted using only those.

What 0.122 vs 0.118 means: with just 29 questions, the correlation was 0.122 — slightly beating all 985 hand-built Eng1000 features at 0.118. Most of the signal lives in a small, nameable subset of questions, which is exactly what makes the embedding interpretable in practice, not just in principle.

Distilled RoBERTa agreement — tap to explain88.5%

What it measures: distillation — training a small, cheap model (RoBERTa) to copy the big model’s yes/no answers, then checking how often the small model gives the same answer as the big one.

What 88.5% means: the small model agreed with the big model 88.5% of the time. Answering ~674 questions with a large model for every piece of text is expensive; a small copier that is right nearly nine times in ten makes the approach practical.

Distilled correlations — tap to explain0.111 / 0.113 vs 0.114

What it measures: whether the cheap distilled model keeps the expensive model’s brain-prediction power. Two flavours were tried: binary answers (plain yes/no) and probabilistic answers (how confident the small model is).

What the numbers mean: the distilled models scored 0.111 (binary) and 0.113 (probabilistic) against 0.114 for the original — the small distilled model kept almost all of the brain-prediction power, losing only a sliver in exchange for being far cheaper to run.

Retrieval MRR — QA-Emb vs BM-25 — tap to explain0.45 vs 0.77 · combined 0.80

What it measures: document ranking, scored with MRR (mean reciprocal rank) — how high the right document lands in a ranked list. 1.0 means the right document is always first; the lower it lands, the lower the score. This is the fMRI paper’s one test that is the same kind of job as our contract search.

What the numbers mean: QA embeddings alone scored 0.45 and lost to BM-25 — plain keyword search — at 0.77. But combining the two scored 0.80, beating either alone. The lesson travels directly to contracts: readable question-based vectors are not a replacement for keyword search, they are a complement to it — each catches matches the other misses.

Same idea, different yardsticks: our 0.906 F1 (v1 0.872) grades yes/no answers against lawyers; their 0.116 grades predictions against a noisy brain signal; MRR grades a ranked list. Keep the yardstick attached to the number and every score on this page is honest — detach it, and none of them are comparable.

7 · Plan of action — what works, what doesn’t, what to expect

This section is written plainly and critically, on purpose. We know this is not a revolutionary paper. Knowing exactly where it is weak is what lets us frame it honestly — and a paper that names its own weaknesses clearly is stronger than one that hides them. Every problem below has a fix next to it. Nothing here is sugarcoated.

Concerns — read these first

These are not footnotes. They are the limits on everything claimed above, and they shape the whole plan below.

  • The pilot is 10 contracts — nothing here is a publishable claim yet. Every number on this page is a pilot-scale hint, not a result you can defend as a finding.
  • The v2 fixes were tuned on the same 10 contracts that exposed the problems. The questions were rewritten because these contracts tripped them — so some of v2’s gain may be fitting the pilot rather than the other 500 contracts. Only the 510 run can tell the difference.
  • Cosine’s weakness is a finding about the method, not a bug to quietly patch. It belongs in the paper, stated openly.
  • There is no retrieval baseline yet — no BM25 / E5 comparison. Without it, the paper cannot claim QA embeddings retrieve better than existing methods.
  • Scaling may drop the numbers. That is expected, not a failure — but it must be reported, not hidden.

What works

  • The answering step. F1 0.906 (v1 0.872), accuracy 0.939, and 96.2% of evidence quotes verbatim. The model can read a contract and answer the 41 questions reliably. This part is solid.
  • Retrieval, with coverage-first ranking. MRR 1.000 — the first right answer at rank 1 on all six queries — and P@3 0.834, both exactly equal to the oracle. In v1 (cosine only) it was MRR 0.89, rank 1 on five of six.
  • The embedding idea itself. Our v2 embeddings retrieve exactly as well as vectors built from the lawyers’ own labels on this test — same P@3, same MRR, same top-3 sets. At pilot scale, the predicted vectors behave like perfect-answer vectors.
  • Interpretability. Every bit is a readable yes/no answer with a quoted clause behind it. The core claim — you can read the embedding and check it — holds.

What doesn’t work

  • Post-Termination Services — fixed, but the fix overshot. Precision 0.33 → 1.00: all 6 false alarms are gone. But recall fell 1.00 → 0.67 — the tightened definition now excludes one genuine case along with the noise. No longer the biggest defect, but not a clean win either.
  • Affiliate License-Licensor is still broken. 0 correct, and still 1 false alarm (was 2). The direction of the licence is still not being read correctly on this side. Its mirror image, Affiliate License-Licensee, was fixed by the same clarification — precision 0.50 → 1.00, no false alarms left.
  • Non-Transferable License got slightly worse. Precision 0.33, recall 0.50 (v1: 0.40 / 1.00) — 2 false alarms and a new miss. The licence-vs-contract note did not land; it is still confused with anti-assignment and sublicensing language.
  • Expiration Date is better, not solved. Recall 0.33 → 0.78, misses down from 6 to 2. Two contracts still phrase their expiry in a way the model does not catch.
  • Contract #9 is still the weakest — 0.854 — and chunking did not help it. Identical accuracy to v1, while contract #10 improved to 0.927 under the same chunking. Whatever is wrong with #9, its length alone was not it.
  • Cosine similarity on sparse binary vectors is still a weak ranker on its own. In v2 it got worse on Q1 — P@3 0.667 — because one matching bit in a sparse contract can still outrank a contract matching everything asked for. Coverage-first ranking is now the rule and fixes the ordering, but anyone reusing these embeddings with plain cosine will hit the same wall.
  • Competitive Restriction Exception picked up new false alarms — 1 in v1, 4 in v2. Nobody rewrote that question; a reminder that changing six definitions can move errors around as well as remove them.
  • P@3 still cannot prove much at pilot scale — ours and the oracle both sit at the 0.834 ceiling, random at 0.75. The 10-contract corpus is too small, and too dense with relevant documents, for P@3 to discriminate.
  • Evidence quotes are still not always verbatim — 5 of 133 are not word-for-word (v1: 6 of 134), despite the stricter quote-or-no rule. For a method whose selling point is checkable evidence, that matters.

What to expect — honest framing

  • This is not a revolutionary paper. It does not beat the best uninterpretable models — Benara et al. were 7% below the best LLaMA, and we do not beat span-extraction baselines on their own task. Anyone who frames it as a breakthrough will be caught out in review.
  • The real contribution is narrower, and it is enough: interpretability, plus a working asymmetric retrieval design, validated end to end at pilot scale. Not a new state of the art.
  • The claim the paper should make is: readable embeddings that retrieve about as well as their own ground truth, with known failure modes. Not “we beat X by Y%”.
  • Scaling to 510 will likely lower the answering F1. Longer, more varied contracts mean more edge cases. New error patterns will appear that the pilot’s 10 contracts did not contain. Expect it. Do not hide it.

Next steps — a fix for each problem, in order

  1. Fix the questions — DONE in v2 (25 Nov 2026). Six definitions rewritten, a word-for-word evidence rule added, and the same 10 contracts re-answered. Results:
    • Post-Termination Services: precision 0.33 → 1.00 — all 6 false alarms gone. But recall 1.00 → 0.67 (1 new miss): fixed, slightly overshot.
    • Expiration Date: recall 0.33 → 0.78 — misses down from 6 to 2.
    • Affiliate License-Licensee: precision 0.50 → 1.00 — fixed. Affiliate License-Licensor: not fixed — still 0 correct, 1 false alarm.
    • Non-Transferable License vs Anti-Assignment: not fixed — slightly worse (precision 0.33, recall 0.50).
    • Evidence: verbatim quotes 95.5% → 96.2% (128/133). Better, not perfect.
  2. Fix the similarity — DONE in v2. Coverage-first ranking replaced cosine-only: match every query bit first, then rank by cosine. The 6-query test was re-run: the oracle ceiling is now MRR 1.000 and P@3 0.834 — the most P@3 allows on this test — and our v2 embeddings match the oracle exactly. Cosine alone remains fragile (v2 Q1 drops to 0.667 under cosine only), so coverage-first is the ranking rule from here on.
  3. Fix the long-contract problem — DONE in v2, with one holdout. Contracts over ~20,000 characters are split into ~20k chunks, answered per chunk, and OR-aggregated — a clause counts as present if any chunk says yes — before building the embedding. Contract #10 improved 0.854 → 0.927. Contract #9 did not move (0.854): chunking was not its problem, and why it lags is still open.
  4. Scale to all 510 — UNBLOCKED, awaiting a go-ahead. The three fixes above were the gate; they are done and measured. The run itself has not started and has not been agreed — it begins only on an explicit go. When it runs: one call per contract, about 7.4M tokens, 30–60 minutes, a few dollars. Then:
    • Re-run answering with the fixed questions and chunking, and score against CUAD ground truth with the same metrics as the pilot.
    • Re-run the retrieval test on a bigger corpus — for example 50 contracts, with more queries — so P@3 can actually discriminate from random.
    • Add baselines: compare against BM25 and a standard dense retriever (E5 / Sentence-BERT) on the same queries. Without a baseline, the paper cannot say whether QA embeddings retrieve better or worse than existing methods. Benara’s MRR 0.45 vs BM-25 0.77 is the warning: QA embeddings alone can lose to plain keywords.
    • Train a small distilled model to mimic the LLM’s answers — like Benara’s RoBERTa, 88.5% agreement — so the method is cheap and reproducible, not dependent on repeated large-model calls.
    • Run the query-side Variant B — LLM-answered query questions — to test the full pipeline end to end, errors on both sides included.
  5. Frame the paper around the weaknesses. Lead with interpretability and end-to-end validation. State the failure modes and the cosine finding openly. Do not claim to beat span-extraction baselines on their task — it is a different task — and do not claim to beat dense retrieval without the baseline run to back it up.
The order mattered — and it was followed. Questions first, then the ranking, then the chunking, then a re-pilot to check — only now is scaling to 510 unblocked. Scaling first would just have produced 510 contracts’ worth of the same known errors, faster. The 510 run still needs an explicit go-ahead; nothing on this page should be read as it having started.

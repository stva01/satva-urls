The data

The pain: every dataset invents its own label scheme — so today, it's one classifier per dataset.

In plain words: these datasets are collections of short posts people wrote during real disasters — “family trapped, water rising”, “bridge collapsed”, “blankets at the community hall” — where a person has read each post and tagged it with exactly one label: what kind of help it is asking for, or reporting. That labelled data exists to solve one problem: mid-disaster, posts arrive by the thousand and no team can read them all. A model that learns from these examples can sort the stream as it arrives, so rescue requests surface first instead of drowning in sympathy messages.

HumAID — the big one

77,000+ posts, labelled by human annotators, drawn from 24 million collected across 19 real disasters (2016–19): hurricanes, earthquakes, floods, wildfires. About 10 labels, all describing what people need.

Role: the largest single source of labelled examples.

CrisisNLP — the shelf

A research lab's collection of earlier labelled sets — one per disaster, each gathered and labelled its own way. Plenty of data, but no two sets use the same labels.

Role: breadth across events — and the labelling mess, in person.

CrisisBench — the merger

Eight existing sets cleaned up, de-duplicated and combined under one label scheme: 141,500 posts for the humanitarian task.

Role: one shared yardstick, so models can finally be compared fairly.

The claim

A single decision model generalises across crisis datasets, taxonomies and languages — without a new model per dataset, at a fraction of LLM latency and cost, and runs offline.

The numbers

Published figures for these models as they run today — the scale of the gap this pitch stands on.

≈40 msper decision — Laya on a basic GPU. On plain CPU, ≈200 ms.
≈250 msper decision — Jev's hosted API, median, measured by third parties.
0.5–3 sper decision — a general LLM API call. Up to ~75× slower than Laya.
$0per decision — Laya is open-source and runs on our own hardware, offline.
$1,000+per million posts — the LLM API bill, at $0.001–0.20 per call.
0.87weighted F1 — best published score on CrisisBench (RoBERTa). The accuracy bar to match.

Put together: at ≈200 ms a decision, one modest 4-core CPU machine sorts ≈20 posts a second — about 1,200 a minute — with no cloud and no bill.

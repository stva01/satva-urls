The data

The pain: every dataset invents its own label scheme — so today, it's one classifier per dataset.

In plain words: these datasets are collections of short posts people wrote during real disasters — “family trapped, water rising”, “bridge collapsed”, “blankets at the community hall” — where a person has read each post and tagged it with exactly one label: what kind of help it is asking for, or reporting. That labelled data exists to solve one problem: mid-disaster, posts arrive by the thousand and no team can read them all. A model that learns from these examples can sort the stream as it arrives, so rescue requests surface first instead of drowning in sympathy messages.

HumAID — the big one

77,000+ posts, labelled by human annotators, drawn from 24 million collected across 19 real disasters (2016–19): hurricanes, earthquakes, floods, wildfires. About 10 labels, all describing what people need.

Role: the largest single source of labelled examples.

CrisisNLP — the shelf

A research lab's collection of earlier labelled sets — one per disaster, each gathered and labelled its own way. Plenty of data, but no two sets use the same labels.

Role: breadth across events — and the labelling mess, in person.

CrisisBench — the merger

Eight existing sets cleaned up, de-duplicated and combined under one label scheme: 141,500 posts for the humanitarian task.

Role: one shared yardstick, so models can finally be compared fairly.

The claim

A single decision model generalises across crisis datasets, taxonomies and languages — without a new model per dataset, at a fraction of LLM latency and cost, and runs offline.

Literature review

Five papers map the field. Each leaves the same gap open.

CrisisBench · ICWSM 2021

Merged 8 crisis datasets into one benchmark — 141.5k humanitarian tweets, comparable at last.

Gap: labels harmonised by hand, once. Every model is tied to that fixed label set — and no latency or cost is reported.

HumAID · ICWSM 2021

~77K human-labelled tweets across 19 disasters. Single-label, organised by event.

Gap: trained baselines only. No testing on unseen events, no confidence routing, no streaming latency.

CrisisTransformers · arXiv 2023

Models pre-trained on crisis text itself, evaluated on 18 datasets — the strongest supervised baseline family.

Gap: still needs training per dataset, per label set — and it's heavier at inference.

McDaniel et al. · arXiv 2024

Zero-shot LLMs — GPT-4o, Gemini 1.5 Flash, Claude 3.5 Sonnet — classifying CrisisBench posts. The closest prior work in spirit: labels defined in the prompt.

Gap: probabilities come out as generated text from slow, costly LLMs — hard to calibrate, and multi-class humanitarian labelling trails supervised models.

Lamsal et al. · arXiv 2025

“Actionable Help”: mini models built for speed — up to 18.6× faster than BERT at matched accuracy.

Gap: the speed still depends on training a model per task, and no real event is replayed as a stream.

Supervised models are accurate but retrain per dataset. Zero-shot LLMs are flexible but slow and costly. Efficiency work is fast but still trained per task. Jev fills the gap — the label set is written as a typed question at call time, probabilities and confidence come back natively, and no per-task training is needed.
